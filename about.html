Learning by building

In spring 2026 I decided the best way to understand a processor was to make one. I designed a 12-instruction ISA, built a five-stage pipelined CPU for it, and got it running on a small FPGA board, counting Fibonacci numbers on two seven-segment displays. Simulation passing turned out to be the easy part. On real hardware, memory came up full of garbage and synthesis crashed on a 65,536-way mux I hadn't meant to build.

That summer I wanted to know how fast I could simulate that same CPU, so I wrote a gate-level simulator in CUDA. My first GPU version was slower than the CPU, and figuring out why taught me more about hardware than any speedup would have.

What I'm doing now

I'm an electrical engineering student at Georgia Tech. In the Sharc Lab I work on 3D FPGAs: chips built from stacked layers of silicon. My project is a logic block that spans two layers, which meant changing a large C++ toolchain I didn't write and proving every change with tests designed to fail when something is wrong.

On the SiliconJackets ASIC team I do the other end of the flow. I took a RISC-V CPU from SystemVerilog through verification, synthesis, place-and-route, timing and LVS to a real layout. And at HackGT 13, my team's posture-tracking wearable won Best Use of TigerData; I built the hardware and firmware.
